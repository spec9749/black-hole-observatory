EXPLORE THE OBSERVATORY

A black hole is a region of spacetime where gravity is so strong that nothing, not even light, can escape to a distant observer. Their existence is established by gravitational-wave detections, stellar orbits, X-ray emission and, since 2019, direct imaging of their shadows.

Drag the black hole

Each background star is shown with the two images a point-mass lens produces. The images sit at θ = ½(β ± √(β² + 4θE²)), where β is the unlensed offset from the lens and θE = √(4GM/c² · Dls/(DlDs)) is the Einstein radius. Model limits: weak-field approximation; real black holes also produce strong-field relativistic images near the photon sphere.

Units G = c = M = 1, so the horizon is at r = 2. Orbits integrate the Schwarzschild equation d²u/dφ² + u = 1/L² + 3u² with u = 1/r. The 3u² term is purely relativistic: it makes elliptical orbits precess and removes stable orbits inside r = 6 (ISCO). Dashed rings mark the photon sphere (r = 3) and ISCO (r = 6).

Schematic, Schwarzschild (non-rotating) geometry; the disk’s outer edge is arbitrary here.

dτ/dt = √(1 − Rs/r)

For a clock held static at radius r, τ is its own elapsed time and t is time far away. Rs = 2GM/c². The rate falls to zero at the horizon. This is the static-observer result; orbiting or falling clocks tick differently, and holding still near a horizon needs an enormous force.

z(r) = 2√(Rs(r − Rs)), Rs = 2GM/c²

This is an embedding diagram (Flamm’s paraboloid): it shows how space is curved in the equatorial plane of a non-rotating black hole at one moment, with r the circumference radius and z the extra height needed to draw that curved surface in flat 3D. The window is fixed at 120 km, so a heavier hole carves a wider, deeper funnel. The amber ring is the horizon, where the funnel turns vertical. Limits: it shows spatial curvature only, not the warping of time (see Time dilation), and the rubber-sheet picture is an analogy, not a literal surface.

Drag to orbit

Units: Rs = 1. The horizon radius shrinks from 1 Rs toward 0.5 Rs as spin rises, and so does the ISCO, which is also the disk’s inner edge. The black disc is the shadow, radius ≈ 2.6 Rs for a non-spinning hole (3√3/2 Rs). Ray-traced Kerr geometry: every pixel follows a light ray backwards through the spinning-hole (Kerr) metric in Kerr–Schild coordinates, integrating Hamilton’s equations for a photon. Frame dragging, the shifted and flattened shadow and the thin photon ring all emerge from the geometry. The disk orbits prograde on circular Kerr orbits and is rendered as an absorbing, emitting volume. A gas element seen through a shift g is again a blackbody, at temperature gT, with g = 1/[ut(1 − Ωλ)] (Ω the gas’s angular speed, λ = Lz/E for the photon). Colour comes from integrating Planck’s law against the CIE 1931 colour-matching functions (Wyman et al. 2013 fit) and converting to sRGB. Untick the Doppler box to remove the shifts.

Temperature: Page–Thorne. The effective temperature follows the full Novikov–Thorne / Page–Thorne (1974) flux of a thin Kerr disk, which carries the relativistic corrections the older Shakura–Sunyaev profile lacks and falls to zero at the ISCO, where the stress vanishes. I checked the closed form against the flux integral derived from first principles (agreement ~10−7) and against the radiative efficiency 1 − EISCO (5.7% at a = 0, 32% at a = 0.998). The slider sets the maximum Teff, a free setting; a real disk’s depends on mass and accretion rate.

Self-illumination (returning radiation). Light from the disk bends around the hole and strikes the disk again (Cunningham 1976). Offline I traced 384,000 photons per spin through the Kerr metric (double precision) from a razor-thin disk with Lambertian emission, recorded where they land, and solved F = FPT + K·F, assuming the absorbed light is fully thermalised and re-emitted (zero albedo). The result is tabulated at 11 spins and interpolated. It heats the inner disk and lets the inner edge glow where FPT is zero. It adds about 2% to the disk’s luminosity at a = 0, 8% at 0.9 and 30% at 0.998, so it matters mainly for rapid spin, consistent with Cunningham. Untick the box to compare. Limits: the table is not re-traced live, the traced disk is infinitely thin and ends at 10 Rs, and reflection, emission lines, the plunging region and illumination of the disk’s own flared surface are omitted. The tracer reproduces the analytic photon-capture impact parameters of Kerr to 4 to 5 digits, and for photons that land on the disk its Hamiltonian constraint and angular momentum stay conserved to better than 10−4 up to a = 0.99 (about 3×10−3 in the worst case at 0.998). Its absolute returning fractions are not benchmarked against published numbers; the trend with spin agrees with Cunningham’s.

Vertical structure. The scale height comes from hydrostatic balance, H = cs/Ω with cs ∝ √Teff: the disk is thin where it is cool, pinches toward the ISCO and flares gently outward (H/r ≈ 0.03 to 0.05 at the default 9000 K, scaling as √T). Density is a Gaussian core plus a thin, tenuous exponential atmosphere (0.6% of the core density, scale height 2.5 H), with vertical optical depth 30 per face. Temperature is stratified by the grey-atmosphere relation T4 = ¾ Teff4(τ + ⅔), integrated through the radiative-transfer solution, so a face-on view looks into hotter layers than a grazing one (limb darkening). Multiplicative, roughly log-normal turbulent density fluctuations are sheared by the disk’s differential rotation. Limits: this is a parametrised model, not a solved vertical-structure calculation. Radiation-pressure flattening, relativistic vertical gravity, magnetic structure, a Comptonised corona, warps and outflows are omitted.

Sky. The background is 5,044 real stars to about magnitude 6 (XHIP, an extended Hipparcos compilation, via d3-celestial), at their catalogue positions and with their B−V colours converted to a temperature and then to a colour by the same Planck–CIE pipeline as the disk. They are painted onto a cube map and seen through the lensing, so Einstein rings, double images and the compressed sky near the shadow come from the geometry. Limits: each star’s size and peak brightness are display scalings, not photometry; there is no Milky Way, no nebulae and nothing fainter than about magnitude 6; the sky’s orientation relative to the hole is arbitrary.

da/dt = −(64/5) G³m1m2(m1+m2) / (c⁵a³)

Two black holes orbit their common centre and radiate gravitational waves, which carries away orbital energy, so the separation a shrinks. The inspiral follows the Peters (1964) quadrupole formula above for a circular orbit. As a falls, the orbit speeds up and the wave signal climbs in both pitch and strength, the “chirp” that LIGO–Virgo–KAGRA detect. Below about a = 6 GM/c² (the ISCO for the total mass) the orbit is no longer stable and the pair plunge together. The merged hole then rings down at a frequency set by its mass and spin, ≈ [1.5251 − 1.1568(1 − af)0.1292] / (2πGMf/c³) (Berti, Cardoso & Will 2006). The defaults, 36 + 29 M☉, are close to GW150914 (Abbott et al. 2016, PRL 116, 061102), which ended as a ≈ 62 M☉ hole after radiating about 3 M☉c².

Model limits: schematic after the ISCO. Real mergers need numerical relativity, and here the plunge is a drawn path rather than a solution of Einstein’s equations. Hole sizes are the Schwarzschild radii 2Gm/c² drawn to scale, but positions are Newtonian-style coordinates. The animation is slower than reality and stretched near the end so you can follow it; the readout gives the true frequency and duration. Remnant spin uses the Buonanno–Kidder–Lehner (2008) fit for non-spinning, circular binaries. The radiated-energy fraction is my own interpolation between the test-mass limit and the equal-mass numerical-relativity value (≈ 4.8%), so treat it as approximate. The wave plot has arbitrary amplitude, and the expanding rings are a simplified picture of the radiation, which really has a two-lobed pattern. Neither hole has spin or eccentricity here.

What we still don’t know

  • What happens at the singularity; classical relativity breaks down there and no tested quantum-gravity theory replaces it.
  • Whether information survives black hole evaporation.
  • How supermassive holes grew so large so early in cosmic history.
  • Whether intermediate-mass holes are common, and how they form.
  • Whether cosmic censorship holds in general.
  • Whether primordial black holes exist at all.
